OLTP Product Data Modeling — Salesforce Senior Data Modeler

Built Fri 2026-07-31 for the Sat 2026-08-01 session · Interview Tue 2026-08-04 · Salesforce T&P Data Modeling Team

0. What this role is, in one paragraph

You are not modeling a warehouse and you are not administering an org. This team designs the logical data model inside Salesforce's products — the entities and relationships that engineers then implement, across Sales, Service, industry clouds and Data 360. You sit with PMs, UX and architects as a design authority: sketch the ERD, surface risks, drive to approval, defend the trade-offs.

Therefore the likely interview shape: a live logical modeling session where the interviewer plays a PM describing a feature vaguely, and watches how you interrogate, sketch, surface risk, and defend. The 6 mocks run on 7/23 were exactly this shape. Talking out loud is not optional here — it is the evaluated skill.

The trade-off vocabulary, straight from the JD

They name the axes a design must balance: stability, architecture, user experience, performance, speed to market, software cost of ownership. Use these words. When you defend a decision, name which axis you optimised and which you sacrificed. That single habit reads as "design authority" rather than "engineer with opinions."

1. Cold check — run first (5 min)

Do not read ahead. These are asked verbally at the start of the session.

  1. A telecom subscriber's plan_type changes and invoices must still report under the old plan. Name the technique and list the columns.
  2. An order is placed, packed, shipped, delivered. One table or four? Why?
Bar: (1) says "SCD Type 2" unprompted, and names both the surrogate key and the natural key that stays the same on every version — the piece missed on 7/31. (2) says "accumulating snapshot" — one row, milestone timestamp columns, updated in place.

2. Normalization — the product-design version

Nobody in this interview wants the textbook definitions. They want to hear why a form breaks if you skip a step. Learn it as three questions you ask of a table:

FormQuestionWhat breaks if you skip it
1NFDoes any column hold more than one value?Comma-separated phone_numbers. Can't index, can't validate, can't ask "who else has this number." Every filter becomes a LIKE scan.
2NFDoes every non-key column depend on the whole composite key?On OrderLine(order_id, product_id), storing order_date — it depends on order_id alone. Now the date is repeated on every line and two lines can disagree.
3NFDoes any non-key column depend on another non-key column?Storing account_id and account_industry on Contact. Industry belongs to the account. Update it in one place, ten contacts now lie.
The one-liner to have ready: "Normalization is about making each fact live in exactly one place, so there is exactly one place to change it." Anomalies (insert / update / delete) are just the symptoms of that rule being broken.

The three anomalies, with a concrete instance each

  • Insert anomaly — you can't record a new product until someone orders it, because product data lives on the order line.
  • Update anomaly — a customer changes address; you update 400 order rows and miss 3. Now the data disagrees with itself.
  • Delete anomaly — the last order for a product is deleted and the product ceases to exist.

When to denormalize in OLTP (the check never answered on 7/23 — expect it)

OLTP defaults to normalized. You break it deliberately, and you must be able to say what you're paying:

Legitimate reasonExampleThe cost you accept
Read path is hot and the join is on the critical pathDenormalized account_name onto Case for a list view rendered millions of times/dayMust maintain it on rename — trigger, job, or accept staleness
Point-in-time truth is requiredCopying unit_price onto the order line at time of saleNone — this is not denormalization, it's a genuinely different fact ("price charged" ≠ "price now"). Say this out loud; it's a classic trap.
Aggregate needed transactionallyRoll-up counters (open cases per account)Write contention on the parent row; needs careful concurrency design
"I'd normalize by default and denormalize on evidence — a measured read path, not a guess. And I'd write down who owns keeping the copy correct, because an unowned duplicate is how a model rots."

3. Keys

TermMeaningWatch for
Natural / business keyIdentifier the business already uses (email, SSN, order number)They change. People change email. Countries reissue IDs. Never make one the PK if it can change.
Surrogate keySystem-generated, meaningless, immutableDefault PK choice. Salesforce does this everywhere — the 15/18-char record Id.
Candidate keyAny column set that uniquely identifies a rowThe ones you don't pick as PK still deserve UNIQUE constraints — that's where real data quality lives.
Composite keyUniqueness across several columnsCommon on junction entities. Still usually give it a surrogate PK plus a unique constraint on the pair.
Answer to have ready: "Surrogate primary key, always. Natural keys become UNIQUE constraints. That way identity never changes even when the business identifier does — and every FK in the model is insulated from business change."

4. Relationships — the actual core of this job

4.1 Cardinality and optionality

Most candidates state cardinality (1:M) and stop. Optionality is where the design decisions live, and it's what a PM never tells you unprompted.

QuestionDesign consequence
Can a Contact exist with no Account?FK nullable or not — and whether "orphan" is a valid business state
Must every Order have at least one line?Can't be enforced by FK; needs application/transaction logic. Say so — knowing the limits of declarative constraints is a senior signal.
What happens to children when the parent is deleted?Cascade vs restrict vs orphan — this is exactly master-detail vs lookup in Salesforce
Can it change over time?Reparenting allowed? Do you need history of the relationship (which makes the relationship an entity)?

4.2 One-to-one — usually wrong, sometimes right

Default assumption: a genuine 1:1 means the two entities should be one table. Justify it or merge it. Legitimate reasons to keep them apart:

  • Optionality — the extension exists for only 5% of rows (a Person Account's individual attributes)
  • Security — sensitive columns split so access can be granted separately (compensation, SSN)
  • Volatility / size — a large blob or a hot-updated counter kept off the main row to reduce contention and row width
  • Lifecycle — the two halves are created and retired at different times by different systems

4.3 Many-to-many — resolve with an associative entity

Student ──< Enrollment >── CourseSection
              |
              +-- enrolled_on, status, grade, waitlist_position
The tell that decides everything: if the join carries any attribute of its own, it is not plumbing — it is a real entity with its own name and its own lifecycle. Name it after the relationship as a noun (Enrollment, Assignment, Membership), never StudentCourse.

Follow-up they may push on: "can the same student enroll in the same section twice?" If no → unique constraint on the pair. If yes (re-taking a course in a later term) → the term is part of the key, and you just discovered a missing dimension of the grain.

4.4 Self-referencing relationships

Account hierarchies, org charts, product bundles, case parent/child. Model as an FK back to the same table (parent_account_id).

  • Ask: is it a strict tree, or can a node have multiple parents? Multiple parents = M:M against itself = an associative entity, not a self-FK.
  • Ask: how deep, and are cycles possible? Depth affects whether recursive traversal is viable at query time.
  • Risk to surface: nothing in a plain FK prevents A→B→C→A. Cycle prevention is application logic.

4.5 Polymorphic relationships — very Salesforce

"A Task can relate to a Lead or a Contact" (WhoId), "and to an Account, Opportunity or Case" (WhatId). Three ways to model it:

OptionShapeTrade-off
Multiple nullable FKslead_id, contact_id, both nullable, exactly one populatedReferential integrity preserved ✅, but a new target type means a schema change ❌
Type + id pairrelated_to_type + related_to_idExtensible ✅, but the database can no longer enforce the FK ❌ — integrity moves to the application
Supertype tableAll targets share a common parent entity; FK points at the parentIntegrity ✅ and extensible ✅, at the cost of an extra join and a shared key space
"I'd want to know whether the set of target types is open or closed. Closed and small — separate FKs, keep the integrity. Open and growing — a supertype, so I keep the constraint instead of pushing it into application code."

4.6 Supertype / subtype — the single most Salesforce-relevant pattern

Party model: Account is sometimes an organization, sometimes a person. Same for Payment (card / ACH / wire), or Product (physical / subscription / service).

StrategyShapeWhen
Single table + type discriminatorOne wide table, record_type column, irrelevant columns NULLSubtypes differ by only a few attributes; you query across all types constantly. This is essentially what Salesforce Record Types do.
Table per subtypeSeparate tables, no shared parentSubtypes share almost nothing and are never queried together
Supertype + subtype tablesCommon attributes on the parent, specifics on children, shared PKSubstantial shared behaviour and substantial differences — the usual right answer at enterprise scale
The question that decides it: "do users ever need a single list containing all of them?" If yes, the common attributes must live in one place.

4.7 When the relationship itself needs history

"Who owned this account, and when?" The moment history is required, the relationship stops being an FK and becomes an entity with effective_from / effective_to — an OLTP cousin of SCD Type 2. Listen for the words "used to be", "at the time", "audit", "as of".

5. Designing for extensibility — they ship to every industry on earth

This team's whole problem is that the same model has to serve financial services, health, manufacturing and retail. Have an opinion on how you'd extend a model without forking it:

ApproachGoodBad
Add nullable columns per industrySimple, typed, indexableTable sprawls; 90% NULL; every industry's concerns leak into a shared object
Extension table per industry (1:1)Isolates concerns; core stays cleanExtra join; proliferation of tables
EAV / metadata-drivenInfinitely extensible without DDLNo typing, no constraints, poor query performance — unless the platform is built for it
High-value prep read (1 hour, worth it): Salesforce's own multi-tenant architecture whitepaper. Their platform stores every tenant's custom objects and fields in a shared, metadata-driven schema with pivot-table indexing — i.e. they made the "bad" EAV option work at enormous scale, deliberately. Being able to say "you've industrialised the metadata-driven approach, which is why custom fields don't require DDL per tenant" puts you ahead of almost every candidate. Verify the current details before quoting specifics.

7. How to run the design session itself

The discovery questions (ask before you draw anything)

  1. Who are the users and what are they trying to do? — the entity list falls out of the verbs
  2. Walk me through one real example, end to end — concrete instances expose cardinality that abstractions hide
  3. What's the volume and the access pattern? — 100 rows vs 100M changes everything downstream
  4. Can it be zero? Can it be many? Can it change? — optionality, cardinality, mutability, for every relationship
  5. Does anyone need to know what it used to be? — the history question; ask it early, it restructures the model
  6. Which of these already exists in the Salesforce model? — reusing Account/Contact beats inventing a new object, and this JD explicitly asks you to map into existing models
  7. What's out of scope for v1? — speed to market is a named axis; design the seam, don't build past it
The behavior change from 7/31 — this is the one thing to fix: before naming anything, say two sentences out loud — "What happens here is ___." and "One row per ___." Slowly. Every miss on 7/31 came from answering on instinct first. The reasoning was never wrong when it was actually run.

Surfacing risk like a design authority

Say the risk, the consequence, and the mitigation — in that order, in one sentence.

"If we let this be a free-text field, we can't report on it in six months and we'll be migrating dirty data under pressure. I'd make it a reference entity now — it costs us a day and it's reversible later, whereas the free-text version isn't."

8. Live mocks — Saturday

Scenarios only. Do not read the spoiler blocks before the session.

Mock 1 — Field Service scheduling

"We want customers to be able to book an appointment with a technician. Not every technician can do every job — you need the right skills and the right certifications. Techs work in territories and have working hours. Design the data model."
Expected coverage: WorkOrder (the customer's request) vs ServiceAppointment (the scheduled slot) as separate entities — one work order can need several visits. ServiceResource (technician) ──< ResourceSkill >── Skill as a resolved M:M carrying proficiency level and certification expiry (the attribute that proves it's a real entity). WorkOrder ──< WorkOrderSkillRequirement. Availability modelled as recurring working hours + exceptions, not as pre-generated slot rows. Territory as a dimension with a self-hierarchy.

Risks she should surface: double-booking is a concurrency problem no FK prevents; certification expiry means a valid assignment can become invalid with the passage of time alone; appointment lifecycle (scheduled → dispatched → in progress → completed) is status on one row, not four tables — the accumulating-snapshot instinct in OLTP clothing.

Traps: collapsing WorkOrder and ServiceAppointment into one table; making skills a comma-separated field (1NF); generating a row per technician per 15-minute slot forever.

Mock 2 — Households for Financial Services Cloud

"Advisors need to see a household — a married couple, their kids, maybe a trust — and roll up total assets across the household. People move between households after a divorce. Design it."
Expected coverage: Household as its own entity, not an attribute on Contact. Contact ──< HouseholdMember >── Household, with role (primary / spouse / dependent / trustee), effective_from, effective_to — because membership changes and advisors must see history. Contact-to-Contact relationships (spouse of, parent of) as a self-referencing M:M with a role, and the reciprocal-record question ("if A is spouse of B, does B automatically get a row?"). Asset rollup: a maintained aggregate vs computed on read — name the trade-off explicitly (staleness vs query cost).

The senior signal: recognising that "people move between households" makes the relationship temporal, so the join table needs effective dating — the OLTP sibling of SCD Type 2. Also: can a person be in two households simultaneously (a child of divorced parents)? That kills any one-household-per-contact shortcut.

Traps: household_id as a column on Contact; no history, so divorce silently rewrites the past; modelling relationships as picklist text.

9. Positioning — what to say about your own background

The stated bar is 10+ years of relational (OLTP) design. Don't try to manufacture that, and don't apologise for it either — they granted the interview knowing your profile. Compete on design judgement demonstrated live, which is what the session actually measures.

Anchor stories to reach for, framed as design authority, not implementation:

  • Citi (DCRM / Data Acquisition Platform) — logical and physical models, views ingesting Kafka topics into Redis. The story is whose requirements you reconciled and what you traded off, not the pipeline.
  • IQVIA — healthcare domain, HIPAA/GDPR constraints shaping the model. Regulatory constraint is a design axis; this JD lists industries including health.
  • ClinicalFlow — end-to-end ownership with explicit de-identification and consent modelling.
Your free differentiator: the JD makes AI-assisted modeling a pillar of the role and says they want to improve those capabilities. You genuinely work this way daily (Claude Code, AI-assisted development). Have one concrete story: what you used it for, where it was wrong, how you caught it. "Where it was wrong and how I caught it" is the part that separates enthusiasm from judgement — and it's exactly what a design-authority role needs to hear.
