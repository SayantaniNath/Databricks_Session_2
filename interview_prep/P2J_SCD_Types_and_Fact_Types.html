1. Is it a business process?

A business process is something the business does — a verb, captured by an operational system, happening at a point in time and producing numbers. Three tests:

TestQuestionVerdict
TimestampDoes it happen at a moment, or does it just exist?Happens → process. Just is → dimension.
MeasuresDoes the event produce numbers worth summing or counting?Yes → fact table.
GrainIs this a new grain, or the same row at a later time?Same grain later → milestone (a column), not a new table.

From a confirmed process you derive four things: the fact table (named after the event), the grain (one row per what), the measures (numbers the event produced), and the dimensions (the who/what/where/when hanging off it).

Carry rule: A new fact table needs a different grain, not a later timestamp.

2. The three fact table types

The discriminator is what causes a row to exist — not how often it happens.

TypeRows are born fromOne-question testAdditivity
TransactionAn eventNothing happened yesterday → no row for yesterdayFully additive
Periodic snapshotThe clock/calendarNothing happened yesterday → row anywaySemi-additive (sum across products ✅, across days ❌)
Accumulating snapshotA lifecycleOne row per thing, updated in place as milestones completeMilestone timestamps + lag measures
Misconception corrected 7/31: "anything that happens frequently is a transaction fact." Frequency is irrelevant. A stock-price snapshot taken every second is periodic; a contract renewal once a year is a transaction.

Worked example — inventory (the two-sibling case)

SKU-A has 500 on hand. Mar 1 a truck brings 200. Nothing Mar 2–5. Mar 6 a truck brings 50.

FactInventoryReceipt — transaction, 2 rows. Mar 2–5 do not exist in this table:

Mar 1 | SKU-A | WH-1 | 200
Mar 6 | SKU-A | WH-1 |  50

FactInventorySnapshot — periodic, 6 rows, one per day regardless:

Mar 1 | 700    Mar 4 | 700
Mar 2 | 700    Mar 5 | 700
Mar 3 | 700    Mar 6 | 750

Sum the receipt column across days = 250 received, meaningful. Sum the snapshot column across days = 4,250, meaningless. Only the calendar-driven table has the semi-additive problem.

The stock having a value is not the same as an event occurring. Apply the test to the table you are designing, not to the warehouse.

3. SCD Type 2 — the mechanism

Scenario. Priya covers Northeast. In March she transfers to West. Comp analytics needs every deal closed before March to still roll up under Northeast, and every deal after under West. Same employee ID.

You do not update her row. You close it and open a new one.

DimSalesRep — before:

rep_key | employee_id | region    | effective_from | effective_to | is_current
501     | E-8842      | Northeast | 2023-01-01     | 9999-12-31   | TRUE

After the 15 March transfer — two rows:

rep_key | employee_id | region    | effective_from | effective_to | is_current
501     | E-8842      | Northeast | 2023-01-01     | 2026-03-14   | FALSE
502     | E-8842      | West      | 2026-03-15     | 9999-12-31   | TRUE
ColumnJob it does
rep_keySurrogate key — a new one per version. This is what makes the design work.
employee_idNatural/business key — identical on both rows. It's what says "these two rows are one person."
effective_from / effective_toThe window in which this version was true. Open-ended row carries 9999-12-31.
is_currentFlag ETL uses to find the live row quickly.

Optional additions: version_number, row_hash (for change detection), audit timestamps.

What the ETL does on the change date

  1. Detect the change (row hash or column compare against the current row).
  2. UPDATE the old row: set effective_to = change date, is_current = FALSE.
  3. INSERT a new row with a new surrogate key, effective_from = change date, effective_to = 9999-12-31, is_current = TRUE.

Update-then-insert. Never overwrite the attribute in place.

The payoff — why it's automatic

A January deal stored rep_key = 501 on the fact; an April deal stores 502. Comp analytics simply joins fact → dim on the surrogate key and history comes out correct — no date logic, and no filtering on is_current. The fact froze the correct version at load time.

Diagnostic to remember: if a historical report has to filter is_current to produce the right number, something upstream is already broken — most likely the fact stored the natural key instead of the surrogate key.

Fact-side lookup rules

  • Normal load: look up the dimension on the natural key where is_current = TRUE, store that surrogate key on the fact.
  • Late-arriving fact: look up instead with event_date BETWEEN effective_from AND effective_to. Range joins belong in ETL backfill/repair — not in consumer queries.

4. Type 1 vs Type 2 vs Type 3

TypeWhat it doesUse when
Type 1Overwrite in place; history lostThe old value was simply wrong (a correction), or nobody analyzes by the attribute
Type 2New row per version; full history keptThe old value was right at the time and past facts must still roll up to it
Type 3prior_value column on the same row; one step of historyOnly the immediately previous value is needed, side by side with current

Discriminator to say out loud in an interview: "Is the old value wrong, or was it right at the time?" Wrong → Type 1. Right at the time → Type 2.

2b. Structural difference — what the three fact tables actually look like

The tell: how many date keys the table has, and whether rows get updated.

Transaction — one date key, insert-only:

FactOrderLine
order_date_key | product_key | store_key | customer_key
order_id (degenerate) | line_number
quantity | sales_amount | discount_amount        <- fully additive

Rows born from events, never updated, grows with business volume.

Periodic snapshot — one date key, insert-only, rows born from the calendar:

FactInventorySnapshot
snapshot_date_key | product_key | warehouse_key
on_hand_qty | on_hand_value | units_received_today   <- semi-additive

Every SKU × warehouse × day gets a row whether or not anything moved. Predictable size = dimensions × periods.

Accumulating snapshot — many date keys, rows get UPDATEd:

FactOrderFulfillment
order_key (one row per order)
order_date_key | packed_date_key | shipped_date_key | delivered_date_key
customer_key | warehouse_key | current_status_key
order_amount
days_order_to_ship | days_ship_to_deliver | days_total   <- lag measures

Inserted when the order is placed, later date keys pointing at a "not yet happened" row in the date dimension; each milestone rewrites the row.

TransactionPeriodicAccumulating
Rows born froman eventthe clocka lifecycle starting
Date keys11many (one per milestone)
Write patternINSERT onlyINSERT onlyINSERT then UPDATE
Grows withvolumedimensions × periodslifecycle instances
Measuresadditivesemi-additivedurations, lags, status
Typical question"how much did we sell Tuesday?""what was on hand Tuesday?""how long from order to delivery?"
Two lines worth saying in an interview: accumulating snapshots are the only fact type you routinely update — which is why Delta MERGE INTO shows up in these pipelines and why they are awkward on append-only storage. And if you are adding a fourth date key to a transaction fact, you actually have an accumulating snapshot.

2c. Column vs table — the cardinality test

1:1 with the row → column. 0-to-many with its own measures → its own table.
"It happens later" never decides it. Settlement happens later and is a column; a chargeback happens later and is a table. The difference is cardinality, not the clock.

Card payments worked example. FactPayment = accumulating snapshot, one row per payment attempt, auth_ts / capture_ts / settle_ts as columns, updated in place — auth and settle are the same money, exactly 1:1. FactChargeback = separate transaction fact: most payments have zero, some have one, some are disputed twice or charged back for a partial amount with its own reason code and outcome. A column cannot hold "two chargebacks," and you would carry millions of NULLs.

4b. Additivity — additive / semi-additive / non-additive

TypeSums across…ExamplesHow to handle
Additiveevery dimension, time includedsales_amount, quantity sold, receiptsSUM anywhere
Semi-additiveevery dimension except timeon-hand inventory, account balance, headcount, subscribersAcross time use AVG / LAST / point-in-time, not SUM
Non-additivenothingoccupancy rate, margin %, conversion rate, unit priceStore numerator and denominator as additive facts; compute the ratio at query time

700 units Mon + 700 Tue ≠ 1,400 — same stock counted twice (semi-additive). 40% + 60% ≠ 100% (non-additive). Periodic snapshots are the classic semi-additive table.

Why this kills bad table names: you cannot store a ratio at a grain. FactOccupancyRate / FactRevPAR are impossible tables — you store the countable thing that produces both halves (FactRoomNight) and divide at query time.

4c. Deriving the grain — reusable formula

Grain = the composite unique key of the fact table, stated in business language.

  1. Name the event (verb, not measure) → that's the table name.
  2. Ask: what is the smallest thing that produced one row? → the atomic entity.
  3. List the dimensions you must fix to land on exactly one row — dimensions only, never measures.
  4. Say: "one row per ⟨A⟩ per ⟨B⟩ per ⟨C⟩."

Two checks: Countable? — the sentence should let someone compute the row count (3M meters × 96 fifteen-minute intervals = 288M rows/day). Low enough? — if they asked "which procedures," claim-header grain fails.

Rule of thumb: go to the most atomic level the source gives you. You can always roll up; you can never roll down.

4d. Fact-table naming — the trap and the fix

The failure shape (7/23 and again 7/31): naming the table after the number the executive asked for instead of the thing that happened.
ScenarioWrong (derived measure)Right (the event)Grain
Smart metersFactMeterConsumption, FactUsageFactMeterReadingone row per meter per 15-minute interval
Hotel occupancyFactRevenue, FactOccupancyRate, FactRevPARFactRoomNightone row per room per property per night, occupied or vacant
Airline OTPFactAirlinePerformance, FactDelay, FactFlightScheduleFactFlightLegone row per leg per scheduled departure date
Naming rule to carry: the fact table is named after the verb in the sentence, never the noun in the metric. Finish "one row per ___" first — then name the table after that thing.

The mechanical motion (taught 7/31 after the 4th repeat slip)

The table name is the noun phrase at the end of your own grain sentence. Do not invent a name — read it off the grain you just said.

Grain sentenceName
one row per meter per 15-min readingFactMeterReading
one row per room per nightFactRoomNight
one row per flight leg per dateFactFlightLeg
one row per member per class bookingFactClassBooking
one row per claim lineFactClaimLine

Deriving the name in three questions (worked on the airline case)

  1. Cross out every metric word in the question. "on-time performance", "delay causes" — both are analysis, neither ever happens, so neither can name a table. If little is left, the question handed you metrics only and you must supply the event yourself.
  2. What physically happened? A plane pushed back at SFO, flew, landed at ORD. Past tense, real-world verb: a flight operated.
  3. "One row per ___?" Per flight number? No — one row for all history. Per flight number per date? Closer. But UA328 may fly SFO→ORD→JFK — one number, two hops, each with its own delay. Atomic unit = the leg. → one row per flight leg per scheduled departure date → FactFlightLeg.

Keep the denominator. Vacant hotel rooms get rows; on-time flights get rows. FactAirlineDelay would contain only late flights, so on-time rate becomes uncomputable. Filtering the denominator out of existence is the same non-additive mistake one layer down.

Legitimate second facts (different grain, therefore different table): FactCustomerBill alongside meter readings; FactReservation alongside room-nights — one booking of 3 nights = 1 reservation row but 3 room-night rows, and only reservations know booking lead time.

5b. Process vs dimension vs milestone — the three tests, and the rapid-fire results

TestQuestionVerdict
Noun vs verbSay it as a sentence — what's the verb?No verb → dimension. An insurance policy exists; a policy being issued happens.
Parent testA milestone is always a stage of something already in flight. What row would it be a stage of?No parent at the same grain → it's a process.
CardinalityHow many per parent?1:1 → column (milestone). 0-to-many with own measures → own table (process).

Careful with the parent test: a plausible parent still has to survive the grain check. Enrollment feels like a stage of admission — but admission is one row per student per program per term, while enrollment is one row per student per course section per term. One admission, five enrollments → different grain → own table.

6. Interview quick-fire

QuestionAnswer in one breath
How do you preserve history on a changing dimension attribute?SCD Type 2 — close the old row (effective_to, is_current=FALSE), insert a new row with a new surrogate key; natural key unchanged.
What ties the two rows together?The natural/business key. The surrogate key is what makes them different.
How does a report get historically correct numbers?The fact stored the surrogate key current at event time — plain join, no date logic, no is_current filter.
Transaction vs periodic snapshot?If nothing happened yesterday: no row → transaction; row anyway → periodic.
When is Type 2 the wrong answer?Attribute nobody groups by (Type 1); attribute churning faster than facts arrive (mini-dimension / Type 4); only one prior value needed (Type 3); source has no reliable change detection.
Four delivery-stage timestamps — one table or four?One. Accumulating snapshot, same grain, updated in place. A new fact table needs a different grain, not a later timestamp.
