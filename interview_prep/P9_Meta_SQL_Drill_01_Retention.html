Meta SQL Drill 01 — Retention

Session: Wednesday 2026-08-05 · Meta DS Product Analytics screen prep (Fri 2026-09-04) · Week 1, Day 1

Rules — non-negotiable, these are the point of the drill.
  1. Complete, runnable query. From a blank editor. Every time. No fragments, no "and then I'd join…". Meta's own guide: "pseudo-code that allows you to gloss over details is not acceptable."
  2. Clock on. Q1 = 6 min. Q2 = 8 min. Q3 = 10 min. When time's up, stop and look — don't keep polishing.
  3. Say the clarifying question out loud before you type. Each problem below has at least one genuine ambiguity. Finding it is scored.
  4. No peeking at solutions until all three are written. A wrong complete query is worth more than a right fragment.

The schema

Two tables — the standard Meta screen setup. Presto/ANSI SQL.

users
  user_id       BIGINT
  signup_date   DATE
  country       VARCHAR
  platform      VARCHAR    -- 'ios' | 'android' | 'web'

events
  user_id       BIGINT
  event_date    DATE
  event_name    VARCHAR    -- 'open_app' | 'view_reel' | 'like' | 'comment' | 'share'
  session_id    BIGINT

Assume: events can have many rows per user per day. A user may have zero rows in events. Today's date is 2026-08-05. Data ends yesterday, 2026-08-04.

Q1 — D7 retention (6 min)

Of the users who signed up in July 2026, what percentage were still active on day 7 after signup?

Before you type — the ambiguity to raise: "day 7" can mean exactly the 7th day (classic/bracket retention) or any activity in days 1–7 (rolling/range retention). They mean different numbers. Ask which, then say which you're implementing. For this one, implement exactly day 7.

Solution + traps 
SELECT
    100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT e.user_id) / COUNT(DISTINCT u.user_id) AS d7_retention_pct
FROM users u
LEFT JOIN events e
       ON e.user_id   = u.user_id
      AND e.event_date = u.signup_date + INTERVAL '7' DAY
WHERE u.signup_date >= DATE '2026-07-01'
  AND u.signup_date <  DATE '2026-08-01';

Traps in this one:

  • Count users, not events. COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) on both sides. COUNT(*) in the numerator counts a user with 40 likes as 40 retained users. This is the single most common miss.
  • The date condition belongs in the ON clause, not the WHERE. Put it in WHERE and the LEFT JOIN silently becomes an INNER JOIN — your denominator collapses to only retained users and you get 100%.
  • Integer division. 100.0, not 100. You've hit this before.
  • Half-open date range. >= Jul 1 AND < Aug 1, not BETWEEN … AND '2026-07-31' — safe against timestamps if the column type ever changes.

You don't strictly need the LEFT JOIN here (an inner join + a separate denominator count works too), but the LEFT JOIN version generalises to Q2 with no rewrite, which is why it's the one to have in your fingers.

The follow-up they will ask: "Is this number right?" — No. Users who signed up on July 30 have not had 7 days yet if data ends Aug 4… actually they have (Aug 6 > Aug 4 — check it). Any cohort whose day-7 falls after 2026-08-04 is censored and must be excluded from the denominator, or your retention is artificially depressed. Add AND u.signup_date <= DATE '2026-07-28' and say why out loud. This censoring point is the discriminator on retention questions. Raise it before they do.

Q2 — D1 / D7 / D28 by weekly cohort (8 min)

For users who signed up in June and July 2026, produce one row per signup week showing cohort size and D1, D7 and D28 retention rates.

Ambiguity to raise: same classic-vs-rolling question — use exactly day N again for consistency. Also ask whether weeks start Monday or Sunday.

Solution + traps 
WITH first_touch AS (
    SELECT
        u.user_id,
        u.signup_date,
        DATE_TRUNC('week', u.signup_date) AS cohort_week,
        e.event_date
    FROM users u
    LEFT JOIN events e
           ON e.user_id = u.user_id
    WHERE u.signup_date >= DATE '2026-06-01'
      AND u.signup_date <  DATE '2026-08-01'
)
SELECT
    cohort_week,
    COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) AS cohort_size,
    100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN DATE_DIFF('day', signup_date, event_date) = 1
                                THEN user_id END) / COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) AS d1_pct,
    100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN DATE_DIFF('day', signup_date, event_date) = 7
                                THEN user_id END) / COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) AS d7_pct,
    100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN DATE_DIFF('day', signup_date, event_date) = 28
                                THEN user_id END) / COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) AS d28_pct
FROM first_touch
GROUP BY cohort_week
ORDER BY cohort_week;

Traps:

  • COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN … THEN user_id END) is the whole pattern. Learn this shape — it is the workhorse of every Meta funnel and retention question. SUM(CASE WHEN … THEN 1 END) counts events, not users, and is wrong here.
  • The LEFT JOIN must stay outside any date filter on event_date, or unretained users vanish from cohort_size.
  • Censoring again, and worse: D28 for the late-July cohorts is not observable yet. Either exclude those cohorts from the D28 column or return NULL for it. Say this out loud — a candidate who reports a D28 of 4% for a cohort that's 6 days old has failed the round even with perfect syntax.
  • Dialect: Presto is DATE_DIFF('day', a, b); Postgres would be (b - a). Say which you're writing and move on — Meta does not grade dialect.

Q3 — Rolling retention by platform, with a size floor (10 min)

For users who signed up in July 2026, what share came back at any point during days 1–7 after signup? Break it out by platform, and only include platform-cohorts with at least 100 signups. Order by retention, worst first.
Solution + traps 
WITH cohort AS (
    SELECT user_id, signup_date, platform
    FROM users
    WHERE signup_date >= DATE '2026-07-01'
      AND signup_date <  DATE '2026-08-01'
      AND signup_date <= DATE '2026-07-28'      -- day-7 must be observable
),
returned AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT c.user_id
    FROM cohort c
    JOIN events e
      ON e.user_id = c.user_id
     AND e.event_date >  c.signup_date
     AND e.event_date <= c.signup_date + INTERVAL '7' DAY
)
SELECT
    c.platform,
    COUNT(DISTINCT c.user_id)                                        AS cohort_size,
    100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT r.user_id) / COUNT(DISTINCT c.user_id)    AS rolling_d7_pct
FROM cohort c
LEFT JOIN returned r ON r.user_id = c.user_id
GROUP BY c.platform
HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT c.user_id) >= 100
ORDER BY rolling_d7_pct ASC;

Traps:

  • > signup_date, not >=. Signup-day activity is not retention — nearly every user is active on day 0. Including it inflates the number toward 100% and is the classic silent error here.
  • The size floor goes in HAVING, not WHERE — it's a condition on an aggregate.
  • Censoring is baked in via the <= 2026-07-28 line. Point at it and explain it unprompted.
  • Note the number will be higher than Q1's — rolling (any of days 1–7) always ≥ classic (exactly day 7). If yours isn't, you have a bug. Say this comparison out loud; it shows you understand what you computed rather than just producing it.
The mid-round pivot they will throw at you (Meta explicitly scores "adapting code based on new information"): "Now do it for existing users instead of new users — what share of users active in a given week are still active the following week?"

That's a different query entirely: no users table anchor, no signup date. It's a self-join of weekly-active users against themselves on week + 1. Sketch it out loud in one sentence before writing — "aggregate events to distinct user-weeks, then self-join that on week = week + 1, and divide." Don't start typing until you've said the shape.

Debrief — answer these after, in writing

  1. Which of the three did you finish inside the clock?
  2. Did you raise the classic-vs-rolling ambiguity before typing, on your own?
  3. Did you raise censoring on your own, or did you read it here first?
  4. Where did you stall — recalling syntax, or deciding the shape of the query? (Different fixes: syntax = more reps; shape = say the sentence out loud first.)
The one habit this drill is really training: say the shape of the query in one plain sentence before you touch the keyboard. "Left join users to events on a 7-day offset, count distinct users on both sides, divide." Your drill logs say your reasoning is right whenever you actually run it — the failure is starting to type before the reasoning has run. Same fix as the fact-naming drills, different surface.
